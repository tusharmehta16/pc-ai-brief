Brown & Brown launches proprietary AI advisory platform COMPASS

Brown & Brown announced COMPASS, an AI and analytics ecosystem built on its own data and specialised knowledge, citing a Fortune 50 client case where restructuring cut premium spend by 65 percent while reducing risk volatility.

So whatA major broker is positioning proprietary analytics as its core value proposition in program design, which shifts leverage in placement conversations and raises the bar for what carriers must supply in data and transparency to compete for the same accounts.
DeeperThe headline figure is the 65 percent premium cut for a Fortune 50 client, but that is a single case study from the broker's own marketing, not an audited or generalisable result, and no detail is given on what retentions, limits, or risk transfer structure changed to produce it. The more durable number is the Employee Benefits claim: customers using Health Plan Intelligence saw an average five year cost trend under 4 percent versus an industry norm of 7 to 9 percent, which at least implies a repeatable book-level effect rather than one outlier. Steve Hearn and Powell Brown frame COMPASS as connecting previously siloed capabilities rather than adopting a generic model, and the firm has partnerships with Anthropic, McKinsey and Accenture behind it. By end of 2026 Brown & Brown expects COMPASS-powered knowledge assistants available to thousands of staff, starting in the US. For a carrier, the exposure is competitive rather than direct: if large brokers can show clients quantified premium and volatility outcomes from their own analytics, carriers lose some control of the underwriting narrative in broker-negotiated placements. What remains unproven is whether COMPASS's outcomes hold outside flagship case studies or across smaller accounts where data density is lower. This is vendor narrative dressed as a platform launch, and should be read as broker competitive positioning first, technology proof second.
Watch next: Whether Brown & Brown or a peer broker publishes COMPASS-driven outcomes across a book of accounts rather than single case studies.

Intact and Aviva tell brokers to govern staff AI use now

At the IBAO 2026 convention, Intact's Peter Janzen and Aviva's Paul Fletcher told Ontario brokers to adopt AI for narrow operational problems while putting controls around how their own staff use it.

So whatCarriers are effectively asking distribution partners to extend AI governance down the value chain, which means a carrier's own vendor and broker oversight framework needs to cover broker-side AI use, not just internal use.
DeeperJanzen called the industry still in the first inning and warned brokers to be wary of glossy LinkedIn pitches promising to clean up back offices, saying Intact's own 1,200 person AI lab finds results only come from narrow, specific administrative problems solved one at a time. Fletcher went further on exposure: he told brokerage owners that if they have 20 staff, one is almost certainly already using AI, and asked directly whether they have a policy governing it, framing this as a trust and data security issue since brokers handle information that is personal to consumers and material to business customers. Fletcher also flagged a second order effect that matters more to underwriting: AI is changing customers' businesses and risk profiles, which is an opening for brokers to re-explain risk to underwriters, implying submissions and risk narratives coming from brokers may increasingly reflect AI-altered client operations. Nothing here is regulatory, but it is executives at two major carriers publicly stating that unmanaged broker-side AI use is now a live operational risk in the distribution chain. No detail was given on how either insurer would enforce this contractually with broker partners.
Watch next: Whether any carrier starts requiring AI use disclosures or policies as a contractual condition in broker agreements.
