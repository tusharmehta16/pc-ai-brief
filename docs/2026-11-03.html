Only 5% of Americans accept AI-only claims decisions

Pew Research surveyed 3,488 US adults in June and found 60% uncomfortable with AI aiding a doctor's diagnosis, only 5% comfortable with insurance claim decisions made by AI without human review, and nearly half wanting no AI role in those decisions at all.

So whatPublic trust limits how far a carrier can push claims automation regardless of the internal efficiency numbers vendors are reporting, and this becomes the disclosure and governance constraint on every deployment decision in this brief.
DeeperEileen Yam of Pew, who led the report, said Americans 'generally don't want AI operating solo' and 'want a human in the loop doing final reviews.' The data also shows patients trust doctors more than insurers, government, or tech firms to use AI in their interest, which places carriers low in the public trust hierarchy for any AI-assisted decision. More than half of respondents see AI as a risk to their personal health information, and the negative shift since Pew's 2022 survey was sharpest among younger adults, undercutting the assumption that generational turnover will ease resistance. The survey is framed around healthcare broadly rather than P&C claims specifically, so there is no line of sight into whether sentiment differs for property damage versus injury or health-adjacent claims. This bears directly on how a carrier positions AI-embedded tools like the ones Gore Mutual and Vertafore are rolling out today: backoffice automation that never touches the customer-facing decision may be tolerated, but any move toward AI-assisted adjudication needs explicit human review and disclosure to avoid reputational exposure. Nothing here is regulatory yet, but the data gives any state regulator a ready justification for disclosure mandates on AI use in claims.
Watch next: Any state insurance regulator or the NAIC citing this trust gap to propose AI claims disclosure requirements.

Hiscox scales AI quoting after 75% speed gain

Hiscox is widening its AI-augmented submission-to-quote tool across Retail after it cut speed to quote by around 75% in two US broker lines.

So whatFaster submission-to-quote cycles raise the competitive floor on speed to bind in broker-distributed lines and force every carrier still running manual triage to answer for the gap.
DeeperThe only figure given is 'around 75% faster' across two unnamed US broker lines, with no baseline cycle time, no bind ratio data, and no mention of hit ratio or loss ratio impact. That is a speed claim, not a profitability claim, and the two are not the same thing in underwriting. The decision to widen rollout across Retail suggests Hiscox is confident enough in the result to scale it, but nothing in the release says the 75% figure held once volume and line diversity increased. This sits alongside Vertafore's own internally tested efficiency claim today, both self-reported by the vendor or carrier with no third-party validation, which is the pattern to watch across this whole wave of agentic underwriting tools. What is unproven is whether faster quotes are changing bind rates, retention, or adverse selection, none of which a speed metric alone can tell you.
Watch next: Any disclosure from Hiscox of bind ratio, retention, or loss ratio movement tied to the widened rollout.

Vertafore agent cuts MGA policy change review time 70%

Vertafore launched Velocity Change Request Agent, which it says reduced time spent reviewing and documenting policy changes by 70% and cut errors by 98% in internal testing, built on the same Velocity AI Platform as its earlier Submission Processing Agent.

So whatAutomating endorsement and renewal comparison shifts MGA underwriter time from clerical line-by-line checking toward risk judgment, but the headline numbers are Vertafore's own internal test results, not independently verified.
DeeperEmily McGinn, general manager of MGA and Wholesale at Vertafore, framed the pitch directly: 'Underwriting judgment is the scarcest resource in an MGA, and too much of it goes to comparison work technology can handle.' Mechanically, the agent reads incoming change requests, compares them against the existing policy, and presents differences in plain language, with the underwriter reviewing and editing before anything saves to the record, so the human-in-the-loop design here is consistent with what Pew's survey today says the public actually wants. This is now the sixth Velocity AI agent Vertafore has shipped for MGAs this year, which signals a platform land grab rather than a one-off feature. The 70% time reduction and 98% error reduction are internal testing figures with no stated sample size, line of business, or MGA customer attached, so treat them the same way as Hiscox's 75% figure: directionally credible, not independently proven at scale. Unknown is how this performs under real transaction volume and whether the error reduction holds for complex multi-line policy changes versus simple endorsements.

Gore Mutual embeds AI assistant in core claims and policy systems

Gore Mutual deployed Guidewire ProNavigator across its Claims and Personal Lines underwriting operations, embedding the AI assistant directly in its core policy and claims applications.

So whatEmbedding AI inside the core system of record, rather than as a standalone copilot, means AI output now flows directly into policy and claims records, which raises the bar on model governance and audit trail requirements.
DeeperThe release gives no performance metrics at all, no speed figure, no error rate, no accuracy benchmark, which makes this the thinnest of today's three underwriting and claims deployments by evidentiary standard. What makes it notable is architecture, not numbers: embedding directly in core applications versus a bolt-on tool is a materially different governance posture, since every AI-assisted suggestion now sits inside the system that generates the policyholder-facing decision. That is the exact scenario Pew's survey today warns carriers about, since claims decisioning is where public tolerance for AI is lowest. Nothing in the release says whether a human review gate exists before an AI-generated claims or underwriting suggestion is finalized, which is the first question worth asking Guidewire or Gore Mutual directly. This also connects to the open question in today's set about whether paperwork misreads by AI get caught before they affect a record, a live concern given a thin story today flagging exactly that risk.
